B · Overlap only  ← my pick

The cream is laid on top.

Nothing pins and nothing moves at a different speed. The cream section just starts 46 pixels early and carries a soft shadow along its top edge, so it sits on the photograph instead of after it.

This is the whole idea of layered cloth without any of the theatre. You will feel it and not notice it, which for a shop is exactly right.

Two CSS lines: margin-top -46px and a shadow. No JavaScript, no sticky, nothing to go wrong on an old phone.

The photo waits while the cream covers it.

Now the hero pins and the collection travels up over the top of it. The photo gets a moment to be looked at, and the products arrive as a deliberate act rather than a scroll position.

Used once, at the top of the homepage only, I think this earns its keep. Used at every section boundary it would become the website's personality, and that isn't what you want the personality to be.

D · Hold and settle

And the photo gives way.

The same hold, but the photograph drifts up a little and dims as the cream covers it — the way the underneath layer falls into shadow when you lay cloth over it.

This is the far end. It is the most convincing as an effect and the most likely to feel like a website showing off, because it needs JavaScript on every scroll frame to do something a shadow was already implying.

Sticky hero, plus one scroll handler setting a translateY and an opacity on a brown veil. The only take here that costs you anything.

Read

B everywhere, C once.

B is two lines of CSS and it does ninety per cent of the work. Put it on every boundary where a cream section meets a photograph or a colour band and the whole site quietly gains a sense of being made of pieces laid down.

Then C on the homepage hero and nowhere else, so there is exactly one moment on the site where something holds still and waits for you. D I would leave alone — it spends JavaScript on every scroll frame to say something the shadow in B already says.
